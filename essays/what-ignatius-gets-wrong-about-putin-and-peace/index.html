What Ignatius Gets Wrong About Putin and Peace

Russia has paid heavily for the war. That does not make Putin more likely to accept peace.

David Ignatius sees an encouraging sign in the war in Ukraine: American negotiators have assembled a framework for a settlement. His November 19 column describes progress on security guarantees, European integration and territorial compromise. These proposals reflect a wider sense of urgency in Western policy debates, as officials and analysts seek credible pathways to end the conflict without sacrificing core principles of European security or Ukrainian sovereignty. These are consequential subjects. But a framework becomes a route to peace only when the participants have reasons to accept and sustain the outcome it proposes [1].

The central question is what would make Vladimir Putin accept the lasting failure of his effort to subordinate Ukraine. Ignatius acknowledges that Putin may be using negotiations to prolong a war he still intends to win. That possibility deserves to govern the assessment of the talks. In his column, it is still a qualification to an otherwise hopeful account [1].

Nearly five years after the full-scale invasion, Russia has paid an extraordinary price without achieving its central political objective. In July, the Center for Strategic and International Studies estimated approximately 1.4 million Russian battlefield casualties, including killed, wounded and missing personnel, through June 2026. Its analysis also documented painfully slow advances and recent territorial reversals. The Kremlin has continued fighting through those costs [2].

The temptation is to assume that this makes compromise increasingly attractive. It may instead make an outcome that looks like failure increasingly dangerous to the man who ordered the invasion. During the Nixon administration, the political and reputational costs of rapid withdrawal helped sustain a protracted war in Vietnam despite mounting costs. The cases are not equivalent. The narrower point is that leaders do not necessarily treat further loss as the greater danger. Sometimes the political meaning of retreat becomes the danger [3].

The cost of the war to Russia and the cost of ending it to Putin are different calculations.

That is the paradox this diplomatic optimism underestimates. The longer the war continues, the more Putin may need an outcome large enough to justify what it has consumed. Every additional year can add to the losses he must explain, the promises he must redeem and the interests his government must accommodate. His objectives need not become larger for compromise to become harder. The political price of accepting less can rise even as his ability to achieve more deteriorates.

This is a judgment about incentives, not a prediction that defeat would automatically remove Putin from office. John Lough’s analysis for Chatham House identifies precisely the danger that an inconclusive ceasefire could expose the gap between the Kremlin’s promises and its achievements, weakening Putin’s authority. He also recognizes that Putin could find a ceasefire attractive if it helped him pursue his political objectives by other means. Both possibilities demand more scrutiny than assurances that the parties privately understand the need for compromise [4].

The economy adds another complication. SIPRI estimated that Russia’s federal budget funding of the war and other military spending reached roughly 7.5 percent of GDP in 2025. Redirecting those resources would involve competing demands from the armed forces and the civilian economy. Russia could continue substantial military spending after a ceasefire, including to rebuild its forces. The economic argument therefore cuts both ways: peace could offer relief, while a pause could also improve Russia’s capacity to resume coercion. In this context, Western sanctions and economic aid become central instruments of leverage. Maintaining or adjusting sanctions could help ensure that Russia faces meaningful costs for renewed aggression, while targeted aid to Ukraine remains vital for recovery and deterrence. The credibility and conditionality of these measures will shape postwar incentives, influencing whether Russia perceives restraint or renewed confrontation as its most viable course [5].

The history need not occupy much space. Putin’s rise was bound up with the second Chechen war. The political system he led subsequently fought Georgia, annexed Crimea and intervened in the Donbas before the full-scale invasion of 2022. These conflicts differed, but they establish his repeated willingness to use force for political ends. An assessment of his intentions should begin with that record [6].

The issue reaches beyond whether Moscow can retain another district of Ukraine. Russia’s December 2021 draft NATO agreement demanded that 1997 serve as the baseline for NATO deployments, that the alliance end further enlargement—including Ukrainian membership—and that NATO conduct no military activity in Ukraine, other Eastern European states, the South Caucasus or Central Asia. Those demands concerned the political independence and security choices of an entire region [7].

Their reappearance in the present negotiations matters. Writing on November 19, Carnegie’s Alexander Baunov argued that the talks had broadened from ending hostilities in Ukraine to revising Europe’s security order, while Moscow retained its territorial claims. In that reading, a more extensive negotiating agenda reflects an expansion of what Putin hopes to gain from the process. It provides little reason to conclude that he is accepting less [8].

The underlying ambition is to recover the authority of a great power entitled to negotiate over the futures of less powerful states. Recreating the Soviet Union’s institutions is unnecessary to that project. A recognized Russian veto over its neighbors’ security arrangements would accomplish much of what matters.

Ukraine is where that claim must fail. An independent Ukraine able to defend itself and choose its own political alignment would establish a limit on Russian power that no agreement among larger countries could erase.

Ignatius’s strongest proposal deserves to be judged against that standard. Ukrainian NATO membership, backed by credible military commitments, could constitute a serious strategic defeat for Putin even if territorial questions remained unresolved. A settlement achieving that result would deserve serious consideration by Ukrainians and their allies.

The evidence that Moscow is approaching such an outcome is still weak. On November 20, the day after Ignatius’s column appeared, Reuters reported that Deputy Foreign Minister Sergei Ryabkov rejected what he described as any form of Ukrainian “quasi-membership” in NATO. Public statements do not settle what might be negotiable in private. They do, however, undercut confidence that the central disagreement has been resolved [9].

Nor does describing a guarantee as similar to Article 5 establish its credibility. Article 5 obliges every ally to assist, but leaves each to decide what action it “deems necessary,” including whether to use armed force. A separate guarantee for Ukraine would be credible only if backed by forces, plans, political authorization, rapid-response mechanisms and visible readiness. The test is not the language of the promise but whether Moscow expects renewed aggression to fail [10].

A promise that Western troops would arrive after Russia moved toward another invasion leaves substantial questions. How would violations be identified? Who would decide that the threshold had been crossed? What forces would be available, and how quickly could they intervene? Sanctions restored after a violation could impose costs. They could not, by themselves, stop an advancing army.

These conditions determine whether a security arrangement changes Putin’s calculation. His signature would matter because of the constraints surrounding it.

There is a danger in treating Ukraine’s strikes and Russia’s attacks as a cycle that diplomacy merely needs to interrupt. Reciprocal violence can obstruct a particular ceasefire, but stopping Ukrainian resistance would not establish Russian willingness to accept Ukrainian independence. The analysis must account for the fighting as well as its destruction.

Negotiations can still achieve valuable results. Prisoner exchanges, humanitarian arrangements and verifiable limits on attacks can save lives. A wider agreement could eventually formalize an equilibrium that Russia has been forced to accept. What talks cannot be presumed to do is create that balance through the ingenuity of their terms.

The objective should therefore be an unambiguous failure of Russia’s attempt to subordinate Ukraine. That requires preserving Ukraine’s capacity to resist and closing off Putin’s expectation that continued fighting, Western exhaustion or a temporary pause will eventually deliver control over Kyiv.

Russian state collapse is neither a prerequisite nor a sensible measure of success. Putin’s departure would not guarantee different ambitions; the security institutions and revisionist pressures that shaped this war would remain. A durable policy must constrain the leadership Russia actually has without assuming that succession will solve the problem.

The same reasoning applies beyond Ukraine. Rewarding coercion would leave dangerous incentives intact in Moldova and the Caucasus, and would sharpen the challenge of deterring pressure against NATO’s eastern members. The circumstances differ, particularly because the Baltic states are NATO members. Further wars are not inevitable. Their prevention requires making aggression demonstrably less useful.

Western governments understandably want this war to end. Its human costs are appalling, its economic costs substantial, and its risks difficult to contain. Ukrainians have the most at stake in deciding what terms they can endure. Their allies should ensure that the urgency of a settlement does not become pressure to accept continued vulnerability.

Sometimes wars have to be won for the peace that follows to last. In Ukraine, that means defeating the attempt to make the country’s sovereignty conditional on Russian permission. Negotiations may eventually record that result. Their value as a path to a durable settlement should be measured by whether they help secure it.

Putin must lose the ability, and ultimately the expectation, of subordinating Ukraine through war. Until there is evidence that his calculation has changed, an elaborate framework is still a proposal for the peace others want him to accept.
