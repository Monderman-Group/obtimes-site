Why the Usual Case Against Socialism No Longer Works

And what capitalist democracies have to do about it

John ElijahNovember 26, 2026Freely accessible

Democratic socialists are winning elections in the United States. The usual answer to them is that socialism has failed wherever it has been tried. That answer has stopped working.

This essay makes four points. The usual argument no longer persuades the people it is aimed at. It fails because it answers a question they are not asking, and because it often arrives as a lecture from people the system has treated well. Socialism is still the worse system, and the record shows it. But people judge by the promises made to them at home. So the people who believe in democratic capitalism have to make it deliver.

This author’s subject is how organizations, including governments, keep or lose the ability to correct their own mistakes. That is the lens here. He believes capitalist democracy is the better system, and this essay shows why. That is the reason its broken promises matter.

The argument that stopped working

The exchange is familiar. One side says socialism failed in Venezuela and everywhere else it was tried. The other side points to Denmark. [1] Both can repeat their lines for years, and neither moves.

The numbers show which side is gaining. In Gallup’s August 2026 poll, 43 percent of adults viewed socialism positively, the highest since Gallup began asking in 2010. Capitalism still leads overall, at 55 percent. Among adults under 35 it trails. There, socialism leads 57 percent to 43. [2] The usual answer was made the whole time, and the number rose anyway.

The voting follows the polling. New York and Seattle elected mayors who call themselves democratic socialists in 2025. Los Angeles and Washington did the same this month. [3] The Democratic Socialists of America, known as DSA, counted about 6,500 members in 2014. It says it passed 120,000 this July. [4] It has two members in the House of Representatives. This month voters elected six more candidates it had backed. [5]

The usual argument often comes as a question. Commentators ask whether a person would rather live in Venezuela or North Korea. That is a false choice. Nobody is being asked to move to Venezuela. People are judging the social contract they were born into, or moved into, and whether it is keeping its promises. That is the benchmark they use. For most Americans, Venezuela, North Korea, and the Soviet Union are abstractions. Most people drawn to democratic socialism have never lived in a socialist country. They would be comparing something they have read about with something they have lived.

The comparison with the past fails the same way. People are told their lives are better than life in the Great Depression. In its larger form, the argument says life is better now than at almost any time in history. [6] Both statements are true, and neither persuades. Nobody lives at any other time in history. The present is the only reference point that counts: what the system says it can and should do, and what it is like to live in it. Nobody was promised a life better than the 1930s. People were promised a fair return on what they put in.

Why it stopped working

The skeptic is comparing the system with its own promise. The promise was simple. Study, work hard, be careful with money, and a person gets a stable job, a home, and a path up the socioeconomic ladder.

Taking part in that contract is only partly a choice. A person can decide not to work, but not for long. A person can skip an education, at a cost. A person can put nothing away for retirement, at a cost. Almost everyone has to pay in. So the fair test is what the contract promised and what it is paying back.

For a long time the formula held. About nine in ten Americans born in 1940 grew up to earn more than their parents did. Of those born in the 1980s, about half do. [7]

Take college. Nearly half of new graduates leave with debt, close to $30,000 on average for those who borrow. [8] For at least a quarter of graduates, New York Fed economists find, the degree does not pay off. [9] More than nine million borrowers are in default on federal student loans. [10]

Take the home. The typical first-time buyer is now 40 years old. In 1991 the typical first-time buyer was 28. [11]

Take the job. In every state but Montana, the default is that an employer can end a job at any time, for almost any reason. [12] The philosopher Elizabeth Anderson calls the American workplace a private government, where the boss gives orders and answers very little to the people who take them. [13] Loyalty is expected to run one way.

Take the taxes. California put nearly $24 billion into housing and homelessness over five years. Its own auditor found that the state had not kept consistent track of what the money achieved. Of five programs the auditor examined, three lacked the data to judge. [14] This author’s book is about that problem: how effort and money get lost inside a bureaucracy before they turn into results. [15]

Now add the lecture. The people who make the usual argument are often people for whom the formula worked. They speak from wealth, power, and success, and they tell the struggling to work harder, be smarter with money, and get an education. That advice is good only while the formula holds, and it is not holding the way it used to. It also leaves out luck. People who succeed tend to give the credit to their own effort. [16] Success does take hard work. Other things help too: a family able to provide support, a favorable year to graduate, and the right moment to buy a house.

So a thirty-year-old with debt, no house, and a job that can end tomorrow is told that Venezuela is worse and that the answer is to try harder. Venezuela is worse. The thirty-year-old already tried.

People in that position are not being won over by a theory of socialism. They are walking away from a promise that was not kept. [17] As the saying goes, fool me once. They will not be fooled a second time.

Why socialism is still the worse system

None of this makes socialism the answer. The DSA platform can be checked against the record, one proposal at a time.

Start with what DSA says it wants. Its 2026 program would take the biggest companies, and the industries the country depends on, into public ownership. It would stop food, energy, medicine, and transportation from being run for profit. It calls for a new constitution with no Senate. It would replace the President and the Supreme Court with “an executive and judiciary chosen by and subordinate to Congress.” [18]

Take ownership first. The program promises democratic control of the companies it would take over. It does not say who would run them or how. [18] The record shows what usually happens. The International Monetary Fund compared about a million firms in 109 countries. State-owned firms were a third less productive than private ones. [19] The reason is simple. A private company that keeps making mistakes goes out of business. A state company gets a subsidy. When a private company is rescued instead, as some were in 2008, people are right to be angry. [20] For a state company, the rescue is routine.

The Soviet Union ran the experiment on food. It took farming out of private hands. The country went from exporting grain to being the world’s largest importer of it. [21] The small plots it left to families covered about 3 percent of the cropland and produced about a quarter of the farm output. [22]

State ownership can work where something outside still checks it. Norway is a capitalist democracy. Its state owns 67 percent of the country’s biggest oil company, and the rest trades on the Oslo and New York stock exchanges. [23]

Venezuela shows what happens without those checks. In 2002 its oil workers joined a strike meant to force the president from office. The strike failed, and the state oil company fired about 18,000 of them, at least four in ten of its staff. [24] Political allies took the experts’ places. Output fell through a decade of high oil prices. It was already 40 percent below its 1998 level when the first American financial sanctions arrived in 2017. Every oil exporter was hit when prices dropped in 2014. Most had saved during the boom. Venezuela had not, and by 2020 its economy had shrunk by about three-quarters. [25] The DSA platform widens state ownership and removes the outside checks in the same document.

Ownership on that scale is also power. When the state owns the biggest employers, the people who write the law sign the paychecks of millions. Venezuela’s oil workers lost a political fight and their jobs with it. In a country with many employers, a person who loses a political fight can still earn a living. Leon Trotsky, a leading Bolshevik, helped build the Soviet Union, where the state was the only employer. In 1936 he described an order that people expelled from the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks) be given no work. In such a country, he wrote, that measure “means death by slow starvation.” [26]

Now the constitution. In many free countries the parliament picks the head of government. That is not socialism, and it is not the danger. The danger is the courts. Denmark, the country democratic socialists like to cite, is a market economy, as its own prime minister reminded Americans in 2015. [27] Its judges answer only to the law, and the government cannot remove them. [28] The DSA program puts judges under the legislature. Whoever wins the single chamber picks the judges and outranks them. That leaves no referee.

DSA says it wants competing parties and proportional elections. [18] Intentions are not the test. No government sets out to build what the Soviet Union and Venezuela became. The design led them there, and a promise on paper is not a check. The Soviet constitution of 1977 put one body above the government and the courts, and it promised independent judges. [29] The Communist Party picked them anyway. [30] Venezuela took the same road by vote. Its 1999 constitution, approved by referendum, ended the Senate. It protected the top court: removing a justice took a two-thirds vote. In 2004 a new law added twelve seats to the court and let a simple majority cancel a justice’s appointment. [31] A bare majority in one chamber walked around the safeguard.

This author has seen where a system with no outside check ends up. He lived in Moscow from 1999 to 2001. Moscow was the capital and the richest city in Russia. Mailboxes hung open off the walls, and a letter could not be relied on to arrive. Housing blocks were in deep decay. Public infrastructure was so run down that it was hazardous and barely usable. Food was in the shops, and many people could not afford it. Unemployment was rampant. People scavenged for food, and homelessness was vast. Other cities were worse.

The rot was decades old. Soviet growth had been fading since the 1970s, and Russians stopped making lasting gains in life expectancy in the mid-1960s. [32] For nearly all of that time no court, newspaper, or election could force the state to fix what was failing. [30] The 1990s made it worse. They did not cause it. Decay that deep is not built in eight years. In 2000 a Russian man could expect to live to 59. [33] The weakness outlived the Soviet Union. [34]

The record is far longer than these cases. For most of recorded history, with rare exceptions, people lived under rulers they could not remove. As late as 1800, no country on earth was a democracy. [35] So there is a great deal of evidence about what unchecked state power does to ordinary life.

Divided countries make the plainest comparison. When Korea was split, the North held most of the coal, the minerals, and the heavy industry. [36] Today income per person in the North is about one twenty-eighth of the South’s, and life is about ten years shorter. [37] East Germany had to build a wall to keep its own people from leaving. [38] Nearly eight million Venezuelans have left their country. [39]

The other side of the ledger is just as factual. The Economist Intelligence Unit rates 26 countries as full democracies, and every one of them has a market economy. [40] Markets do not guarantee freedom. But no full democracy does without them. Nobody has to build a wall to keep people inside Denmark, South Korea, or the United States. On income, length of life, and freedom, the record points one way.

That is the factual case. It is not the point.

A system can be the better one and still not look like the rational choice to a person inside it. The skeptics have lived the broken promise. A person who paid in, was not paid back, and has never lived under a socialist government is not being foolish by looking elsewhere. That person is weighing the evidence at hand. This author can say the alternative is worse because he has stood in its wreckage. Most Americans have not, and they cannot be asked to take it on faith.

Two things are true at once. Capitalist democracy is the better system. It is also not keeping its promises to many of the people who live in it. A fact about Korea does not pay anyone’s rent.

What capitalist democracies have to do

The quality of a system can be judged by how well it delivers on the promises it makes. Socialism promised a communist utopia that was always on the horizon. In 1961 the Soviet Communist Party promised it by 1980. [41] Soviet citizens joked that a horizon is an imaginary line that moves away as you walk toward it. [42] The utopia never arrived, and the outcome was often the opposite. Capitalist democracies have kept their promises more fully at some times than at others, but their record is stronger. This is the moment to look hard at the system at home and ask whether it is doing what it says it is meant to do.

Capitalist democracy can be better than it is. Two things have to change.

The first is to drop the old argument and the lecture that comes with it. Telling people who followed the formula to work harder and be smarter tells them that nobody is listening. Some people drew a good birth and good luck. When they start chiding those who are struggling, it is time for the people at the top to take stock of how far the system has tilted. History shows what follows when they do not. The system that replaces theirs is rarely kind to the people who were on top.

The second is to steward the system so that it does what it says it will do. This is not a case for coddling. It does not mean handouts, housing without work, or income without effort. It is not an argument for higher taxes or lower ones. It means the ordinary bargain holds, so that a person who studies, works, and pays taxes can see what the effort bought.

Stewardship is an easy word to say, so here are two tests. A government should be able to show what its money bought. California could not show it for three of the five programs its own auditor checked. [14] And a path that a society sells to its people should pay off for those who take it. A degree that does not pay off for a quarter of the people who earn it fails that test. [9]

This author would start with the debt. A country should stop sending eighteen-year-olds into adult life owing tens of thousands of dollars on a promise it does not keep for all of them.

The work belongs to the people who believe in democratic capitalism. It is their system to defend, so it is theirs to repair. Chiding from the side repairs nothing.

The rise of democratic socialism is a signal. When wealth and power gather in fewer hands and people feel shut out, they look for an alternative. Right now democratic socialism is the alternative on offer. A democracy with independent courts and many centers of power can still hear that signal and correct itself. That is its real advantage over the system the skeptics are reaching for. It is an advantage only if it is used.

That advantage is being worn down from inside. The United States is not among the 26 full democracies. The same index has rated it a flawed democracy since 2016. [40] Both major parties are reaching for durable power by creative means. Both have redrawn congressional maps in the middle of a decade. [43] Both have moved to add seats to top courts. [44] Each move weakens something that could have told the winner no.

People cannot keep being asked to pay for a future that is either unsustainable or tyrannical. The task is to offer a third one, and to prove it with results.

A conservative commentator made a line famous: “Facts don’t care about your feelings.” [45] More and more, the reverse is true. Feelings don’t care about your facts. Those feelings were earned. They come from what people have lived. The facts say capitalist democracy is the better system. Unless people can feel that in their own lives, the fact will not matter.
