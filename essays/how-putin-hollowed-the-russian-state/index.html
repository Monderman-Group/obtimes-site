How Putin Hollowed the Russian State One Cut at a Time

The closing of Novaya Gazeta, and what a quarter century of thinning did to the state.

John Elijah November 4, 2026 Freely accessible

Novaya Gazeta survived twenty-six years under a ruler not known for tolerance. Its closure is more than the end of a newspaper. It is the final clue to how Vladimir Putin remade the Russian state: not by destroying every institution at once, but by removing, one constituency at a time, the people and bodies capable of contradicting the center. What remains can still command. It can no longer correct itself. The war in Ukraine is what that distinction costs, and the mechanism is not uniquely Russian.

Putin had the power to close Novaya Gazeta as early as 2001. By then it was already the paper that printed what the Kremlin did not want printed. He let it live through 2006, when its best-known reporter, Anna Politkovskaya, who covered the war in Chechnya, was shot dead in the stairwell of her apartment building. He let it live through 2012 and 2014, and even through the first years of the war in Ukraine. Then, in 2026, the space around it finally closed. Police raided the newsroom in April. In September the Justice Ministry labeled the paper a foreign agent, a legal mark that blocks donations and tells readers the state considers it hostile. The paper was ordered out of its building on October 28. On October 30 its editor announced on video that Novaya Gazeta had been killed [1] [2]. Its servers are scheduled to go dark on November 9, when the staff are also to leave the Moscow office they have occupied for twenty-nine years [1]. Why did a man so intolerant of dissent tolerate this newspaper for so long? The answer explains more about Russia than the closure itself.

Part of the answer is symbolism. Novaya Gazeta offered the outside world evidence that Russia still permitted dissent, and closing it carried a price. Mikhail Gorbachev had used some of his Nobel Peace Prize money to finance the paper’s first computers. Its editor, Dmitry Muratov, won the Nobel Peace Prize in 2021 [3] [4]. But the paper was also useful. An independent newspaper is a sensor pointed at a ruler’s own government. If a governor steals from a road budget or a factory poisons a river, every official between the event and the ruler has a reason to keep the news from traveling upward. The paper prints it anyway. The ruler learns what his own people would not tell him. Research on authoritarian states finds the same pattern: governments that depend most on their officials doing their jobs allow the freest press, because the press exposes what officials hide, while governments with oil can afford to stop listening [5]. Russia has oil. It still needs accurate information. The closure suggests that the Kremlin now considers information it does not control more dangerous than the blindness that follows from suppressing it. That is an interpretation, not a fact. The history of the state Putin built is the case for it.

Putin preserved the Russian state’s ability to command while dismantling its ability to correct him. He did not clear the forest at once. He thinned it, one tree at a time, slowly enough that every tree still standing could treat the latest cut as someone else’s problem. The machinery that collects taxes, polices streets, and sends men to the front still works. What has been stripped away is everything that could report to the center independently, refuse the center, act without its permission, or take responsibility for a reversal. The war in Ukraine reveals the price of that trade, but the method long predates the war.

I lived in Russia from 1999 to 2001 and watched the first cut. NTV was the one national television channel that mocked the Kremlin on air and reported the Chechen war as a war. In April 2001 the media arm of Gazprom, the state gas company, took it over. Most of its journalists walked out rather than work for the new management [6]. Few people in business objected; it was a television channel, not an oil company. Two years later Mikhail Khodorkovsky, Russia’s richest man, was taken off his plane on a Siberian runway and arrested. He had been funding opposition parties. Yukos, then the country’s largest oil producer, was broken apart with tax claims until its main assets were in state hands [7]. Few governors objected; this was an oligarch, not a region. The understanding the oligarchs had reached with Putin in 2000 was that they could keep their fortunes if they stayed out of politics, and Khodorkovsky had not [7]. Then, in September 2004, terrorists seized a school in Beslan and more than three hundred people died, most of them children. Within two weeks Putin announced, in the name of a unified state, that governors would no longer be elected by their regions but appointed from Moscow [8]. The journalists who might once have objected had already lost their channel.

The method worked because every cut was narrow enough to isolate its victims. Each one touched a single interest, and everyone else could judge, reasonably, that this was someone else’s problem. By the time the cuts revealed a pattern, many of the groups capable of resisting it were already gone. The same method closed parliament to independents and to any party receiving less than seven percent of the vote in 2007 [9]. It jailed Alexei Navalny, the most prominent opposition politician with a national following, in 2021 and declared his movement extremist; three years later he died in a penal colony above the Arctic Circle [10] [11]. In the last days of 2021 the Supreme Court shut Memorial, the society founded in 1989 to document Stalin’s terror [12]. During the first week of the war, Echo of Moscow and TV Rain, the last major independent radio and television broadcasters, were closed within forty-eight hours of each other [13]. From a distance, it still looked like a forest.

Count what remains and you still find every part of a state: a parliament, courts, parties, governors, a central bank, a constitution. Some institutions have even returned in form. Governors have been elected again since 2012, but candidates must first collect signatures from local deputies who answer to the center [8]. Single-member districts returned to parliament in 2016, but the parliament still passes laws it did not write, often unanimously and within days [9] [14]. The courts still sit, but they acquit well under one percent of the people who come before them [14]. The form returns; the capacity does not. That is what hollow means here. The offices remain, but entry to them and survival within them increasingly depend on the center. By 2003, a quarter of the political elite already came from the military and security services [15]. The result resembles a hub-and-spoke network: easier for the center to control, but increasingly dependent on the center for every consequential act [16]. Stephen Kotkin, the historian of Stalin, uses a plainer description for regimes built this way: they look strong, and they are brittle [17]. The war exposes that brittleness at three decisive moments: before a decision, after a failure, and during a shock.

First, the system could not produce dissent before a decision. On February 21, 2022, three days before the invasion, Putin sat alone at a desk in a Kremlin hall. On live television he called forward the members of his security council, his top ministers, and the heads of the security agencies, one at a time, to endorse a decision already made: recognizing two breakaway regions in eastern Ukraine as independent, the step that opened the way to war. Sergei Naryshkin, the head of Russia’s foreign intelligence service, lost his footing at the lectern. He gave the wrong answer, was corrected from the desk, and supplied the required one [18]. The scene did more than display obedience. It broadcast that no one in the room could challenge the decision.

Second, the system could not correct failure while correction was still cheap. By September 2022 the invasion had stalled, and the Kremlin responded by calling up reservists, Russia’s first mobilization since the Second World War. It was announced on September 21. Within days the road to the Georgian border had become a nine-kilometer line of cars, with waits as long as a day and a half. Direct flights to the countries that still admitted Russians without a visa sold out. Hundreds of thousands of men left the country [19]. The correction was real, but by then it was large enough to trigger the reaction that a smaller, earlier one might have avoided. Officials who could see the front had every incentive to report success. Those able to act on bad news still needed permission.

Clear the forest and what is left is command, and command alone looks strong right up to the day it has to be corrected.

Third, the system could not act on its own during a shock. In June 2023 Yevgeny Prigozhin, whose private army, Wagner, had done much of the hardest fighting in Ukraine and whose fortune depended on the Kremlin, turned his column around and drove it toward Moscow. Along the way it took Rostov-on-Don, a city of a million people, and came within two hundred kilometers of the capital. For most of a day the army, the security services, and the regional governments did almost nothing. Whatever means they possessed, none appeared willing to use them without an order from the center. The standoff was resolved through Belarusian president Alexander Lukashenko [20]. In a system with delegated authority, institutions can respond without waiting for the center to improvise a settlement. Here, the institutions were present; the authority to act was not. Two months later Prigozhin’s plane fell out of the sky [21].

The same structure makes the war easier to continue than to end. Only one person can authorize its conclusion. In a state with no institution able to own a retreat, every retreat belongs to him. Research on dictatorships points in the same direction: leaders who expect defeat to threaten their power or survival have stronger incentives to fight longer [22]. A parliament or a party might once have carried some of the political burden of an exit. Neither can do so now. In an earlier essay I called this condition correction lock: a system can no longer change course cheaply because any correction large enough to work also provokes the resistance that stops it [23].

The consequences extend beyond the battlefield. A state with no institution able to say no has one fewer brake on starting a war. A state with no institution able to own an exit has more difficulty ending one. Agreements with Russia depend unusually heavily on one man, with no autonomous institution positioned to guarantee continuity after him, so partners and adversaries have reason to discount them. None of this is a forecast. Personalist systems can remain stable for decades. Correction lock identifies what a system cannot do, not when it will fail. But the transition this system was built to prevent, the handover of power, is also the transition it is forbidden to prepare for. Institutions are fast to cut and slow to build [24]. Whoever comes next will inherit a state that can carry out orders but cannot correct them.

That brings the argument back to Novaya Gazeta, and to trust. I have written elsewhere that trust comes in two forms [25] [26]. Assumed trust accumulates because an institution has earned it over time. Inferred trust is what people rely on when that history is missing: they withhold trust until evidence earns it, piece by piece. But inference requires evidence, and evidence requires someone independent enough to produce it. That was Novaya Gazeta’s role. It gave Russians information about the state that the state itself did not write. In each of the three moments above, that chain broke. No one in the Kremlin hall could say the decision was wrong. No one at the front was rewarded for reporting that the war had stalled. No institution trusted its own judgment enough to act while an armed column drove toward Moscow. Institutions unable to act independently cannot earn assumed trust. A center that suppresses independent evidence leaves no basis for inferred trust. What remains is obedience, and obedience only tells a ruler what he already believes.

The scale is Russian. The mechanism is not. Every organization is a forest of institutions, some planted deliberately and others grown over time. The trees that matter most are the ones capable of saying no to the person at the top: a board, an auditor, a court, a newspaper, a manager with standing of her own. A leader can clear that forest and gain an unobstructed view. For a while, the quiet may look like trust. It is not. Quiet is what remains when nothing can contradict the center, and an organization that has removed every source of contradiction has also removed its ability to learn anything it does not already believe. Russia has reached that condition at the scale of a state, with a war it cannot end and a succession it cannot plan. Companies reach it at the scale of a product nobody dared call a failure, a merger nobody dared question, or a founder nobody could replace. An organization can correct only what it can hear. Command can carry it a long way. Command cannot tell the person giving the orders when he is wrong.

The week Novaya Gazeta announced its closure, its complete archive—thirty-three years of reporting—was posted to a public code-sharing site beyond the Russian state’s reach [27]. The record left the country. What stays behind is quiet. That quiet is not the absence of a problem. It is the absence of anyone left to report one.
