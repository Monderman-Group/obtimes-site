How Putin Hollowed the Russian State One Cut at a Time

The closing of Novaya Gazeta, and what a quarter century of thinning did to the state.

Jason AdamsonNovember 4, 2026Freely accessible

Novaya Gazeta outlived twenty-six years of a ruler not known for tolerance. This essay asks why, and finds the answer in how Putin built his state: one cut at a time, each one someone else’s problem, until the machinery that carries out orders was all that remained. The war in Ukraine shows what it costs when a state can command but cannot correct itself, and the same loss shows up in companies.

Vladimir Putin could have closed Novaya Gazeta in 2001. It was already the newspaper that printed what the Kremlin did not want printed, and he had the means by then. He could have closed it in 2006, the year its best-known reporter, Anna Politkovskaya, who covered the war in Chechnya, was shot dead in the stairwell of her apartment building. He could have closed it in 2012, or 2014, or on the first day of the war in Ukraine. He waited until 2026. In April the police raided the newsroom. In September the Justice Ministry labeled the paper a foreign agent, a legal mark that blocks donations and tells readers the state considers it hostile. On October 28 the paper was told to leave its building. On October 30 the editor announced on video that Novaya Gazeta had been killed [1] [2]. On November 9 its servers are scheduled to go dark, and the staff are to leave the Moscow office they have occupied for twenty-nine years [1]. That is twenty-six years of tolerance from a man not known for it. The question is why, and the answer explains more about Russia than the closure does.

Here is the short answer. The paper served several purposes. It offered the outside world evidence that Russia still tolerated dissent, and closing it carried a high symbolic price: Mikhail Gorbachev had financed its first computers with his Nobel Peace Prize money, and its editor, Dmitry Muratov, won the Nobel Peace Prize in 2021 [3] [4]. More important, it told Putin things his own officials had reasons to conceal. Think of a paper like this as a sensor pointed at a ruler’s own government. When a governor steals from a road budget or a factory poisons a river, the officials in between have every reason to keep it from the top. The paper prints it, and the ruler learns something his own people would not tell him. Research on authoritarian states finds a pattern: governments that depend most on their officials doing their jobs allow the freest press, because the press catches what the officials hide, and governments with oil can afford to stop listening [5]. Russia has oil. Every state still needs accurate information. What the closure suggests is that the Kremlin has come to regard information it does not control as more dangerous than not knowing. That is a reading, not a fact, and the rest of this essay is the case for it.

The claim is simple. Putin preserved the Russian state’s capacity to command while dismantling its capacity to correct him. He did not clear the forest; he thinned it, one tree at a time, and the slowness let every surviving tree treat the latest cut as someone else’s problem. The machinery that collects taxes, polices streets, and sends men to the front still works. What he removed, one constituency at a time, was everything that could report to the center on its own, refuse the center, act without the center’s permission, or take ownership of a reversal. The war in Ukraine shows what that loss of corrective capacity costs.

This author lived in Russia from 1999 to 2001 and watched the first cut. NTV was the one national television channel that mocked the Kremlin on air and reported the Chechen war as a war. In April 2001 the media arm of Gazprom, the state gas company, took it over, and most of its journalists walked out rather than work for the new management [6]. Few in business objected; it was a television channel, not an oil company. Two years later Mikhail Khodorkovsky, the richest man in Russia, was taken off his plane on a Siberian runway and arrested. He had been funding opposition parties. His company, Yukos, then the country’s biggest oil producer, was broken up with tax claims until its main assets sat in state hands [7]. Few governors objected; it was an oligarch, not a region, and the understanding the oligarchs had reached with Putin in 2000 was that they kept their fortunes if they stayed out of politics, which Khodorkovsky had not [7]. A year after that, in September 2004, terrorists seized a school in the town of Beslan and more than three hundred people died, most of them children. Within two weeks Putin announced that governors would no longer be elected by their regions but appointed from Moscow, in the name of a unified state [8]. The journalists who were left could not object. Their channel was gone.

That is the method. Each cut touched one interest, and the others judged, reasonably, that this one was someone else’s problem. By the time the pattern was undeniable, the groups that could have resisted it were gone. The same method closed the parliament to independents and to any party under seven percent of the vote in 2007 [9]; jailed Alexei Navalny, the most prominent opposition politician with a national following, in 2021 and declared his movement extremist, three years before he died in a penal colony above the Arctic Circle [10] [11]; shut Memorial, the society founded in 1989 to document Stalin’s terror, by order of the Supreme Court in the last days of 2021 [12]; and closed Echo of Moscow and TV Rain, the last major independent radio and television broadcasters, within forty-eight hours of each other in the first week of the war [13]. From a distance, it still looked like a forest.

Count what remains and you still find everything: a parliament, courts, parties, governors, a central bank, a constitution. Some of what was removed has even come back in form. Governors have been elected again since 2012, but a candidate must first collect the signatures of local deputies who answer to the center [8]. Single-member districts returned to the parliament in 2016, and the parliament still passes laws it did not write, often within days and unanimously [9] [14]. The courts sit, and acquit well under one percent of the people who come before them [14]. The form returns; the capacity does not. That is what hollow means here. The offices remain, but access to them and tenure within them increasingly depend on the center. By 2003 a quarter of the political elite had already come out of the military and security services [15]. The resulting system resembles a hub-and-spoke network: easier for the center to control, and increasingly dependent on the center [16]. Stephen Kotkin, the historian of Stalin, has a plainer word for regimes built this way: they look strong and are brittle [17]. What disappeared was not the state’s ability to carry out orders but its ability to challenge, revise, or reverse them. The war reveals that loss in three moments.

The first is dissent before a decision. On February 21, 2022, three days before the invasion, Putin sat alone at a desk in a Kremlin hall and called the members of his security council, his top ministers and the heads of the security agencies, forward one at a time on live television to endorse a decision already made: recognizing two breakaway regions in eastern Ukraine as independent, the step that opened the way to war. Sergei Naryshkin, the head of Russia’s foreign intelligence service, lost his footing at the lectern, gave the wrong answer, was corrected from the desk, and gave the required one [18]. No one in that room could challenge the decision, and the state broadcast the fact.

The second is cheap correction after failure. By September 2022 the invasion had stalled, and the correction the Kremlin chose was to call up reservists, the first mobilization in Russia since the Second World War. It was announced on the twenty-first. Within days the road to the Georgian border was a nine-kilometer line of cars with waits of a day and a half, the direct flights to the countries that still admit Russians without a visa were sold out, and hundreds of thousands of men had left the country [19]. The correction was real, and it set off the reaction a smaller, earlier one would not have. By then, smaller corrections had been deferred. Officials who could see the front had strong incentives to report success, while those able to act on bad news needed permission.

Clear the forest and what is left is command, and command alone looks strong right up to the day it has to be corrected.

The third is an institution acting on its own during a shock. In June 2023 Yevgeny Prigozhin, whose private army, Wagner, had done much of the hardest fighting in Ukraine and who owed his fortune to the Kremlin, turned his column around and drove it toward Moscow. It took Rostov-on-Don, a city of a million people, on the way, and came within two hundred kilometers of the capital. For most of a day the army, the security services, and the regional governments did almost nothing; whatever their means, none of them appeared willing to act without the center’s word. The outcome was negotiated through Belarusian president Alexander Lukashenko [20]. In a system with delegated authority, institutions can respond without waiting for the center to improvise a settlement. Two months later Prigozhin’s plane fell out of the sky [21].

Then there is the war itself, which only one person can authorize an end to. In a state with no institution able to own a retreat, every retreat is his, and the research on dictatorships points one way: leaders who expect defeat to threaten their power or survival have stronger incentives to fight longer [22]. The exit that a parliament or a party could carry, so that no single person wears it, is not available. In an earlier essay this author called this condition correction lock: a system that can no longer fix its course cheaply, because any correction large enough to work sets off the resistance that stops it [23]. For everyone else, the consequences follow. A state with no institution able to say no has one brake fewer on starting a war, and a state with no institution able to own an exit ends one with more difficulty. Agreements with Russia are unusually dependent on one man, with no autonomous institution positioned to guarantee continuity after him, and partners and adversaries alike have reason to discount them accordingly. None of this is a forecast. Personalist systems can remain stable for decades; correction lock identifies what the system cannot do, not when it will fail. But the transition this system is built to prevent, the handover of power, is also the one it is forbidden to prepare for, and institutions are fast to cut and slow to build [24]. Whoever comes next inherits a state that can carry out orders but cannot correct them.

Which brings the argument back to the newspaper, and to trust. This author has written elsewhere that trust comes in two forms [25] [26]. Assumed trust is given freely, because people have learned over time that an institution has earned it. Inferred trust is what people fall back on when that history is missing: they withhold trust until the evidence earns it, piece by piece. Inference needs evidence, and evidence needs someone independent to produce it. That is what a paper like Novaya Gazeta was, a source of evidence about the state that the state did not write. Each of the three moments above is a moment in which no one could infer anything. No one in the hall could tell Putin the decision was wrong. No one at the front was rewarded for reporting that the war had stalled. No institution would act on its own judgment while a column drove toward Moscow. Institutions that cannot act on their own cannot earn assumed trust, and a center that removes independent evidence leaves no way to infer it. What remains is not trust of either kind. It is obedience, and obedience tells a ruler nothing he does not already believe.

The scale is Russian. The mechanism is not. Every organization is a forest of institutions, some planted on purpose and some grown on their own, and the trees that matter most are the ones that can say no to the person at the top: a board, an auditor, a court, a newspaper, a manager with standing of her own. Trust in an organization is assumed when those trees have stood long enough to earn it. It has to be inferred, from evidence, when they have not. A leader can clear the forest and gain a tidy view, and for a while the quiet looks like trust. It is not. Quiet is what is left when nothing can contradict the center, and an organization that has cut down the trees that could contradict it has lost the ability to learn anything it does not already believe. Russia has reached that condition at the scale of a state, with a war it cannot end and a succession it cannot plan. Companies reach it at the scale of a product nobody dared call failing, a merger nobody dared question, a founder nobody could replace. An organization can correct only what it can hear. Clear the forest and what is left is command, and command alone looks strong right up to the day it has to be corrected.

The week the paper announced its closure, its complete archive, thirty-three years of reporting, was posted to a public code-sharing site outside the Russian state’s reach [27]. The record left the country. What stays behind is quiet, and the quiet is not the absence of a problem. It is the absence of anyone left to report one.

About the author

Jason Adamson is the author of Governance, Bureaucracy and Organization: Stewardship, Drift, and Administrative Capacity (Routledge). His governance writing examines how organizations build the capacity to act, how it drifts, and what keeps it. His merit-and-measurement writing examines how institutions measure, sort, and reward people, and what happens when the measures stop working. He holds an M.S. in organization development from Pepperdine University and a bachelor’s degree from the University of Texas at Austin.
