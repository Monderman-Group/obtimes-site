The Predictable Decline of the German Car Industry

Great cars, real costs, and buyers who now have other choices

John ElijahNovember 19, 2026Freely accessible

This author lived in Germany for four years, two blocks from Porsche’s plant in Stuttgart-Zuffenhausen. Nearly every day, trucks pulled out carrying new Porsches. They are exceptional cars. For a growing number of buyers, they are also no longer worth what they cost. The cars did not get worse. Other good cars arrived at lower prices.

That one plant tells the story of the whole German car industry. Its decline was predictable, and almost no one involved did anything wrong. Workers bargained for good pay and shorter hours. Lawmakers answered voters who wanted cleaner air. Regulators wrote rules that protect people. Companies put their money where it earned the most. Each of these was a reasonable answer to a real concern. Each one also added cost to a German car.

A cost is still a cost. Someone else pays it, and that someone can decline. For decades buyers paid, because a German car was better than the alternatives. Now the alternatives are close enough, and many buyers have stopped paying the difference.

This essay is about costs and benefits in a competitive global market for cars. It does not blame workers for acting in their own interest, or politicians for acting for the people who elected them. It assigns blame once, to Volkswagen, for cheating on emissions tests.

For decades, Germany seemed to have it both ways. Its car industry has the highest hourly labor costs in the world [1], and the world lined up to buy its cars anyway. High costs and strong demand lived side by side for so long that it looked like a balance. It was not a balance. It was a delay.

Very clean water can be cooled below its freezing point and stay liquid. It looks like any other glass of water. Tap the glass, and ice spreads through it in seconds [2]. The freeze looks sudden, but the water was below freezing the whole time. The tap set off the freeze. It did not make the water cold. It only ended the delay.

High costs and strong demand pull against each other. When two things like that get along for years, something outside them is keeping the peace. In Germany, it was the buyer who had nowhere better to go. As long as no rival built a car as good, buyers paid whatever the German system cost. In 2015, Reuters put China’s share of the profit at Volkswagen, BMW and Daimler at 30 to 50 percent [3]. That dependence was in plain view, which is why the word predictable fits.

An arrangement like this never holds itself up. It lasts exactly as long as the thing holding it up. Yet every good year gets read as proof that the balance works. Porsche’s best year in China was 2021. Its sales there have fallen every year since [4].

Meanwhile the costs keep growing, and German engineers know why better than anyone. In a machine, every part is allowed a small error, called a tolerance. Each part can pass its own inspection while the small errors add up. Every part is good. The assembly does not fit. Engineers call this tolerance stack-up [5].

Decisions stack up the same way. Better wages were a sound choice. So were cleaner air and careful rules. Each passed its own test, because each was judged alone, by the people it helped. Nobody tested the stack. Call it decision stack-up: sound choices, made one at a time, that add up to a total nobody chose [6]. It does not mean the choices were bad. It means they were only ever judged one at a time.

The buyer judges the whole stack. A buyer in Chicago or Shanghai gets little from German wages or German rules, and compares one car and one price with the car next to it.

One question sorts the stack: does it show up in the car? A skilled worker who builds a better car shows up in the car. A fourth signature on an approval form does not. Costs that show up in the car earn the premium. The rest is accumulated system debt. In 2005, General Motors reported health care expenses of about 1,500 dollars for every vehicle in its North American business [7]. It went bankrupt in 2009.

Now the German stack. Start with labor. One study put it at about 3,300 dollars a car in Germany and about 1,300 in the United States [8]. Workers won that pay through decades of bargaining, which is a union doing its job. Some of it shows up in the car as skill. Some does not.

Emissions rules added more. Under EU law, each carmaker has a target for the average carbon dioxide its new cars emit per kilometer. Each gram per kilometer over the target costs the carmaker 95 euros for every new car it registers in the EU that year. A 2023 law cuts that target by 100 percent, to zero, from 2035 [9]. Porsche alone has spent billions on electric models that have yet to pay off, and lawmakers are now negotiating a looser target that is not yet law [10]. The concern was real. So was the cost.

One cost was not reasonable. Volkswagen rigged about 11 million diesel cars to cheat on emissions tests. The bill passed 30 billion euros and cost the industry part of its reputation [11]. That is the only place in this story where blame belongs.

The last cost matters most. Germany is very good at building administrative systems, each with costs and benefits attached. It is much worse at keeping those systems performing under pressure and competition. A rule is written. A committee is formed. Very few are ever retired. What builds up is administrative debt. It is the price still being paid for a procedure after the reason for it has faded, or after a competitor has found a faster way.

Administrative debt is the purest form of system debt, because none of it shows up in the car. One German research institute estimates that excessive bureaucracy costs the economy up to 146 billion euros a year in lost output [12]. In the car industry’s own 2026 survey, smaller companies named bureaucracy as their biggest problem, and two thirds said they were delaying, canceling or moving abroad investments planned for Germany [13].

The debt sits inside the companies too. By one count, newer carmakers, mainly Chinese, plan on 24 to 30 months to develop a car, and the best keep to it. Some traditional carmakers still take 48 to 54 [14]. This is not about talent. Working in China with a Chinese partner, Volkswagen developed a new car in 24 months [15]. Same company, different system.

This author’s book, Governance, Bureaucracy, and Organization [16], makes the larger case. Administrative systems need stewardship. Someone has to keep them working as conditions change. Without that they drift, and an organization can move only as fast as its administration allows.

Chinese carmakers now offered credible alternatives. In 2019, Chinese brands sold 38 of every 100 cars bought in China. By 2025, they sold 65 [17]. In 2024, the Chinese phone maker Xiaomi launched its first car. It was styled after a Porsche and priced under 30,000 dollars [18]. Xiaomi delivered about 137,000 of them that year, against about 57,000 cars for Porsche in China [19].

China did not cause Germany’s problem. It ended the delay. The buyer in Shanghai who chose the cheaper car was being as reasonable as the worker in Stuttgart who wanted a good wage. That is a rational economic response, and it needs no villain.

The consequences are now visible in jobs and investment. Germany had 691,500 auto jobs in the middle of 2026, the fewest since 2005 [20]. Volkswagen has agreed to cut more than 35,000 jobs in Germany and approved about 50,000 more worldwide, with four German plants under review [21]. In November, Mercedes froze investment in its German plants [22]. Porsche plans to cut about 9,000 jobs, 5,000 of them at Zuffenhausen and Weissach [23].

Seeing this coming did not take supernatural skill or exotic tools. No one had to predict the future. They had to look at the present and ask three plain questions. What is letting these two things live side by side? Who controls it? Is it shrinking? In Germany the answers were visible for years: buyers had no better choice, Germany did not control that, and it was shrinking.

It also takes acting in time. Political writers call the range of choices the public will accept at a given moment the Overton window [24]. Every correction has a window like that. Early, the needed fix is small and people will accept it. Acceptance is not the whole story. In an earlier essay, Can Europe’s Social Model Save Itself? [25], this author described correction lock. It is the point where the fix that is needed is the fix that sets off the backlash that stops it. The window is about what people will agree to. Correction lock is about what the fix sets off once it is tried.

Germany’s car industry is not there yet. It is close. Volkswagen’s board includes the workers’ representatives and the state of Lower Saxony, and it approved the latest cuts without one vote against [21]. That is agreement. The test is what the cuts set off. The decision on the four plants was put off until June 2027 [21], and Volkswagen’s workers are free to strike from January 1, 2027 [26]. Everyone at those tables is defending something real.

There is an easy way out, and it is a trap. Build in cheaper countries, keep the German badge and keep the German price. The industry’s own forecast for 2026 was 4.1 million cars built in Germany and 9.2 million built abroad [27]. BMW built its first electric X3 only in China and shipped it to Europe [28].

Where a car is built is not the problem by itself. BMW has built cars in South Carolina since 1994 [29], and buyers kept paying the premium. The trap is moving to save money, letting the car become ordinary, and still charging for the name. Why would anyone pay a premium for a BMW built in China and designed in Germany when an adequate Chinese car costs less? If the customer pays a premium, the premium has to show up in the product, not only in the logo.

Others have fallen in. In 1981, Cadillac put its badge on a small Chevrolet and charged about twice the Chevrolet’s price. Buyers saw through it [30]. France kept its car brands and moved the work. Its plants built 2.88 million passenger cars in 2000 and 1.06 million in 2025 [31].

Cutting corners is the same trap. In the late 1990s, Mercedes cut costs where customers could see them. It fell from first place in a leading American dependability survey in 1990 to a tie for 26th in 2003 [32]. Money saved on quality is charged to the reputation, and reputation is the greatest asset the German car industry has.

The window is still open. The better exit has five steps.

First, give someone the stack. In a design office, someone adds up the whole chain of tolerances before anything is built [5]. Germany has nobody doing that for costs. Unions handle wages, governments write rules, and companies choose investments. Add up the total every year, and put every cost through one question: does it show up in the car?

Second, pay down administrative debt before anything else. It is the one cut that takes nothing out of the car. That means fewer approval steps inside the companies, and faster permits and less paperwork from government. Every procedure should have an owner and a date by which it must show it is still worth its cost.

Third, settle labor costs early and by agreement, and trade the savings for keeping the work in Germany. In 2026, Porsche’s workers accepted job cuts and smaller bonuses, and Porsche promised to keep Zuffenhausen and Weissach open through 2035 [23].

Fourth, put what is saved into the car. One study finds German firms still leading in driver assistance but Chinese makers ahead in batteries and software [33]. That is where the premium has to be earned again.

Fifth, stay out of the trap. Keep the car German in substance, not only in name, and never cut quality to hit a cost target.

The Porsches this author watched leave Zuffenhausen were exceptional cars. They still are. That is the asset to protect. The way out is not to build them somewhere cheaper under a German badge, or to build them a little worse. It is to stop charging the buyer for things that are not in the car. A cost is still a cost. Someone else pays it. Increasingly, they are declining.
