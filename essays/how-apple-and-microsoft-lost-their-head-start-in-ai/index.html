How Apple and Microsoft Lost Their Head Start in AI

Apple and Microsoft entered the AI race with a head start no startup could match. Apple has more than 2.5 billion devices in active use. [1] Windows runs on 1.6 billion machines every month. [2] Both companies had the money, the engineers, and the customers.

Neither one leads. The Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index is a widely cited scorecard of standard tests. As of late November 2026, its ten top-scoring models all come from Anthropic, OpenAI and Google. [3] Neither Apple nor Microsoft has a model in the top 25 of Arena’s text leaderboard, a public ranking built on millions of user votes. [3] The new Siri runs on models built from Google’s. [4] Microsoft’s Copilot still depends on models from OpenAI and Anthropic. [5]

Neither company has failed. Both remain among the richest companies in the world. But two of the most important technology companies of the last fifty years could not lead in the most important new technology of their time.

This was not fate. Both companies had divisions that had drifted apart or into conflict. Both changed their organization charts years before ChatGPT, and the drift went on. Google had a divided AI effort too. It acted late, paid for an expensive fix, and is now among the leaders. What decided the outcome was when the drift was found and how it was addressed.

This author has described the divisions inside a company as trenches, first in “Trenches, Not Silos” [6] and then in “Hold, Collide, Come Apart.” [7] People in each division work with their heads down. They dig forward. Each trench has its own goals, budget, and rewards. Trenches rarely stay parallel. Some drift apart, until the divisions no longer move in one direction. Some drift toward each other, until two groups fight over the same ground.

In quiet years, this costs little that anyone can see. Revenue comes in. Products ship. The heads of the divisions meet, agree to work together, and go back to digging.

A disruptive technology ends the quiet. It demands people, money, and computing power from several divisions at once, all pointed at one goal. That is when the drift shows. It is also the worst time to find it. Found early, drift is cheap to correct. A shared goal is restated. A reward that sets two groups against each other is changed. A decision gets one clear owner. None of that is a reorganization. Found late, the cheap corrections are gone, and what remains is expensive: a merger of divisions, a new organization, or a check written to an outside company.

Microsoft moved in 2016. It put more than 5,000 people into one AI and research group. [8] Three years later, the company was behind, by its own account. The evidence is a June 2019 email from its chief technology officer to the chief executive and Bill Gates, made public in 2024 in the antitrust case against Google. [9] Its author admits he had not taken Google and OpenAI seriously. When he studied them closely, he found a gap. The talent was there, he wrote, but spread across separate teams, with only a small deep learning team inside each. By his own estimate, the company trailed its rivals by years. His example was a feature in a rival’s product, the autocomplete in Gmail. [9]

This is drift apart. Each team had dug its own small AI effort. The 2016 reorganization had not closed the gap.

The next question is how Microsoft addressed the problem. By 2019, closing the gap was expensive. The month after that email, $1 billion went to OpenAI. [10] Microsoft kept building its own models, but the center of its AI effort moved to an outside company. In March 2024, it put Copilot, Bing and Edge under one leader. [11] The partnership’s terms kept Microsoft from pursuing the most advanced form of AI on its own until the deal was rewritten in late 2025. [10] As of November 2026, it still has no general model of its own among the leaders. [5]

The bet made money. The stake in OpenAI stands at about 24 percent. [10] But owning part of a leader is not the same as being one. And Microsoft had been warned. Mobile phones had taught it the same lesson years earlier, when its strength in personal computers did not carry over. [12]

Apple moved in 2018. It hired Google's head of AI, gave him the company’s AI strategy, and put Siri and its machine learning work under him. [13] That was four years before ChatGPT.

Years of conflict followed between the AI group and the iPhone software group, The Information later reported. [14] This is drift toward conflict: two groups digging toward the same ground. The appointment did not end it.

The cost showed when the technology arrived. A request for more AI chips in 2023 won the chief executive’s approval and then lost more than half of the increase in the finance office, The New York Times reported. Apple’s position is that the team got its chips in the end. [15] June 2024 brought the public promise of a new Siri. Delivery took about eighteen months longer than planned. By March 2025, Siri had a new home under the software chief. [16] Then, in January 2026, came the joint announcement with Google: Gemini underneath Apple’s next generation of models. [4] The reported price is about $1 billion a year. [17]

The company that moved in 2018 now buys the technology from its chief rival in phones. Acting early is not the same as correcting the cause. The expensive fix came anyway: a public reorganization in 2025 and a payment to a rival in 2026.

Google had a divided AI effort too. For nine years it ran two AI labs side by side. Brain was the homegrown one. Google bought DeepMind in 2014 and kept its base in London. The two competed with each other. [18] This author has written about why rival divisions last in “Stalin’s Org Chart.” [19]

Google acted late. ChatGPT arrived at the end of November 2022. [18] The threat to its search business set off an internal emergency. [20] Less than five months after ChatGPT, there was one lab with one leader. [18]

Google’s merger is not a model to copy. A merger is the expensive fix. Google took that step after nine years of separate labs and a rivalry it had not resolved. The merger shows the outcome was open. Google addressed the cause: the split between its two labs. Microsoft made an outside partnership the center of its AI effort. Apple put Siri and its machine learning work under one new leader, and the conflict went on.

Google could act that fast because the parts were already in the building. An AI-first order had gone out to the whole company in 2016, and it had the two labs and chips of its own design. [21] What Google lacked was one direction. Microsoft and Apple turned outside for their leading models.

As of late November 2026, two of the four top-scoring models on the Artificial Analysis index are Google’s. [3]

But Google also shows the price of acting late. The first hit product of the new era belonged to a startup, and it stood on Google’s own research. [18] After a wrong answer in the first demo of Google’s chatbot, its parent lost $100 billion in market value in a day. [22] Google lost the first lap of a race it had every reason to lead. It recovered because its problem could be fixed quickly. No company can count on that.

Two popular ideas say this outcome was never in doubt.

The first is disruption theory. In its common form, it says great companies fail because they are great. They serve their best customers so well that they miss the next thing. The evidence behind it is thin. In 2015, two researchers took 77 cases that the theory’s own books used as proof and put each one to experts in that industry. The full theory matched nine percent of them. [23] A year earlier, a Harvard historian’s critique had centered on handpicked cases. [24] Even the theory’s author did not treat it as fate. His advice to established companies was to build a separate unit around the new technology. [25]

The second is creative destruction. In its common form, it says the old must die so the new can live. The 1942 book that gave the world the phrase says something different about big companies. It names the large company as the main driver of economic progress. [26]

Both ideas describe something real. Companies do miss big changes. But both are used today as if the outcome were decided in advance. That is fatalism, and fatalism excuses the people in charge. If decline is a law, nobody chose it.

The record says somebody chose. Microsoft missed the move to mobile phones, and a new chief executive then turned the company around. [12] Apple made its own hit product, the iPod, obsolete with the iPhone. Google lost the first lap in AI and recovered. A large company can be successful and flexible at the same time. All three of these have been. So when two of them fall behind, size is not the cause. The cause is what they did with what they knew.

A company does not need to predict the next technology. It needs to know, in the quiet years, whether it could respond to one. Are we structurally prepared to make moves if a disruptive technology emerges?

Two tests sit under that question.

The first is when. Does the company check for drift while business is good, or wait until a rival’s product reveals it? Microsoft’s technology chief saw the gap by studying what rivals had already built. Google merged its labs only after ChatGPT. Checking in during quiet years takes attention. Waiting costs the lead.

The second is how. When drift is found, does the company deal with the cause, or cover it? The cause sits in goals, rewards, and who owns each decision. Found early, it can be corrected without moving one box on the organization chart. Moving boxes does not correct it by itself.

Microsoft built one large AI group in 2016. Apple put Siri and its machine learning work under a new leader in 2018. The chart changed both times, and the drift went on. A chart is changed once. Drift has to be watched at all times. This author’s “Fast to Cut, Slow to Build” describes the wider habit: cutting comes easily to a large company, and redirecting people and money does not. [27]

This is a failure of leadership, and the public record makes it hard to excuse. Apple’s proxy filing reports about $193 million in total pay for its six named officers in fiscal 2025. [28] Microsoft reports about $207 million for five officers in fiscal 2026. [29] Together, that is roughly $400 million in one year for eleven people. Most of that pay comes as stock. At that level of pay there is every reason to expect exceptional governance, and the margin for error is very small. These companies have the skill, the talent, and the money to govern themselves that well.

What they lack is a standing practice that works. Their books are reviewed every quarter and audited every year. Both boards report that they oversee strategy. [28] [29] None of it kept the divisions pointed the same way. So the failure is an omission. Attention did not go where attention belonged.

This essay does not argue that any company should last forever. Its claim is more modest. People paid millions a year to run a company can be expected to watch the systems they built, the trenches that drift and converge, while watching is still cheap.

Apple and Microsoft were not too big to lead in AI. Technology change is their business. Their leaders changed the organization chart years before ChatGPT, and the drift went on. The difference was never fate. It was attention.
