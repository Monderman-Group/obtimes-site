Why Big Outages Bunch Together

Unrelated failures can land together. A few shared systems give them a long reach. AI could extend it.

In one week of November 2026, email failed around the world. Every airport in the United States stopped takeoffs. Payroll systems, bank payments and airline flights all had trouble within days of each other. To many people it looked like an attack.

A bad week does not need a mastermind. The public accounts of these failures point to technical trouble, not a coordinated attack.[1] As of this writing on November 17, 2026, some causes remain unsettled. New information could change that assessment.[2][3]

This essay makes three points. Chance produces clusters. Reach can matter more than frequency: one failure at a widely used service can hurt more people than many small failures. And more weeks of widespread disruption are likely as more of daily life depends on digital systems. AI could widen that reach. This is a forecast of growing exposure, not inevitable collapse.

What happened

On Thursday, November 12, Microsoft's email service failed worldwide for about a day. Other services were affected, but Teams chats and meetings worked for most of the outage. A July update had introduced a hidden bug. An automatic shift in online requests, combined with fewer working computers, helped expose it.[2]

The next afternoon, the Federal Aviation Administration, or FAA, halted departures nationwide. It was only the second nationwide ground stop since September 11, 2001.[3][4] Investigators focused on network equipment at two remote sites, including one where a contractor had installed a new router. The final cause remained under investigation.[3]

That evening, some employers lost access to the human resources management system, Workday, which handles payroll, benefits and other staff records. Workday serves more than 11,500 organizations.[5] By the company's own count in 2025, those organizations accounted for more than 75 million users.[6] How many were affected is not public. During a weekly backup check, services at a backup site unexpectedly connected to live records and left them out of sync. Restoration continued into Saturday morning.[7][8]

On Monday, November 16, a software bug at JPMorgan Chase slowed payments. Customers of other banks felt delays when their transactions involved Chase.[9] United Airlines also delayed departures during a roughly 90-minute outage. Crews could not obtain the final aircraft weights needed before takeoff.[10]

Apple's online services failed on November 10 and again on November 17. Apple attributed the second outage to an internal update.[11][12]

The disclosed explanations involved bugs, network equipment, a backup check and an update. None pointed to a coordinated attack. Television discussion nevertheless turned to AI.[13] A crowded calendar can make separate failures look like one event.

Chance comes in bunches

Random does not mean evenly spaced. Toss a coin 100 times, and a run of at least five heads or five tails in a row turns up about 97 percent of the time. Put 23 people in a room, and the odds are better than even that two share a birthday. Nothing has gone wrong with the coin or the room.

Outages can bunch up too. Downdetector, a site that collects reports of service problems, received nearly six million from U.S. users in the seven days ending November 16, its busiest week in a year. Yet its analyst counted three major outages over the past 30 days, against a typical five.[14]

Fewer major events did not mean less harm. A report total measures something different from an outage count. One enormous disruption can dominate it.

How unusual was the week? A rough calculation for this essay suggests that chance alone could produce a week like this every few years. That estimate rests on judgment calls about which failures count as serious and how often such failures normally occur. It shows that chance can produce a crowded week. It does not prove that chance explains this one. Different technical causes also leave room for shared pressures, such as several organizations rushing changes at once.

Reach, not frequency

There is evidence of improving reliability. In May 2026, the Uptime Institute, which tracks outages, reported signs of fewer outages per site for the fifth year in a row.[15] That does not mean every company is improving, or that the total number of failures is falling.

A failed office computer may disrupt one workplace. A failed shared online service can disrupt many organizations at once. Better reliability and greater exposure can exist together.

Microsoft illustrated the difference. By a rough estimate, about six in ten of the week's U.S. problem reports came from that one outage.[14][16]

Chase made the same point another way. Its problem reached people who banked elsewhere.[9]

Picture a town that replaces twenty small bridges with two large ones. The new bridges are stronger and close less often. But when one closes, half the town loses its route to work. Counting closures alone misses the problem. Reach matters.

Why more weeks like this are likely

When serious outages are fairly rare, a modest rise in their number produces a much sharper rise in the chance of a cluster. Several unlucky events get more chances to land together.

A calculation makes the point. Suppose serious outages arrive independently at a steady rate of 19 a year. In about one year out of eleven, at least four of them will fall inside the same seven days. At 37 a year, that happens in about three years out of five. The rate nearly doubles. The chance of such a year grows about six and a half times.

The real numbers are less clear. Uptime describes 15 to 20 serious or severe outages in a typical year. Yet its tables and charts imply around 40 a year from 2023 through 2025.[17][18] That is not proof that the true rate doubled. Changes in reporting and classification complicate the comparison.

So the calculation is an illustration, not a forecast built on a proven trend. Its lesson still holds. If the total number of serious failures rises, clusters can become much more common.

Greater dependence can also turn an ordinary fault into a serious disruption without making the equipment itself less reliable. More digital activity creates more exposure. Better safeguards may offset it. The forecast of more bad weeks rests on the judgment that exposure is likely to grow faster than protection.

Pile-ups and chain reactions

A week can turn bad in two ways. In a pile-up, unrelated failures happen close together. The published accounts are consistent with that explanation for much of this week. No public evidence links Chase's bug to the FAA's equipment problem.[3][9]

In a chain reaction, one failure spreads. At Microsoft, the system sent requests away from failed computers to ones that still worked. Those computers then became overloaded too. A system intended to preserve service helped spread the failure.[2] At the FAA, a flood of data destabilized network equipment and led to a nationwide halt.[3]

More activity that can fail creates more chances for pile-ups. More connections can create more routes for chain reactions. Neither outcome is inevitable. The rate of failure and the strength of the barriers between systems matter too.

What AI adds

AI could increase both exposure and connections. The important change is not simply outages at AI companies. It is what happens when AI agents, programs that carry out tasks with some independence, become part of booking travel, paying bills, keeping schedules and handling records.

Two things could then be true. AI may help test software, monitor systems and catch mistakes sooner. Yet total failures may rise because far more tasks depend on digital systems. A smaller share of a much larger number can still be a bigger number. The balance remains uncertain.

Agents could also pass work between services faster than people can review it. Without effective limits, a mistake in one system could trigger actions in another.

A disputed case shows why those limits matter. The Financial Times reported that an AI coding tool was involved in a 13-hour interruption to one Amazon cloud service in December 2025. Its unnamed sources said engineers had allowed the tool to make changes, and it deleted and rebuilt its working environment.[19]

Amazon said the problem affected one service in one region. It blamed incorrectly assigned access rights, not AI.[20] The case does not establish that an autonomous agent caused this November's failures. It does show why permission to alter a live system matters, whatever tool makes the change.

Why this matters

The public is likely to see more weeks of widespread disruption as more of daily life depends on digital systems. Most such clusters will likely reflect ordinary failures rather than coordinated attacks. That is this essay's judgment, not something the probability calculation can establish.

Public confidence in large technology companies was already low before this week.[21] This author has argued elsewhere that the trust deficit shapes reactions to the companies.[22] A bad week may deepen that distrust, even when the failures have ordinary explanations.

The precise details of each failure are still being worked out. For now, the evidence points to coincidence. Telling a chance cluster apart from a coordinated attack is a useful skill, for security professionals and for everyone else. In the first hours the two can look alike. What separates them is evidence of a link: a shared cause, a shared method or the same actor behind them. So far, no such link has been made public.

Misreading the pattern carries risks in both directions. Treating every cluster as an attack can create needless alarm. Treating every cluster as routine could make a real attack easier to miss.

The useful questions are concrete. What did the service depend on? How many people relied on it? What still worked when it failed?

On the evidence available as of this writing, chance and long reach offer a credible explanation for the week. New findings could change it. The reach is growing, so weeks like this will likely become more common. The lasting risk is not merely that computers fail. It is how much of ordinary life can stop when they do.

Note on the numbers

The calculations use independent arrivals at a constant average rate, a model called a Poisson process. Two million simulated years were checked at each annual rate, 19 and 37. Every rolling seven-day interval was eligible, including intervals crossing a year boundary. The test asked whether at least one qualifying cluster ended within each 365-day year.

For four or more outages, the estimated annual probabilities were 9.25 percent and 60.05 percent. These describe the share of years containing a cluster, not a regular waiting time between clusters. The lower rate is within Uptime's stated range; the higher approximates counts inferred for 2023 and 2024.[17][18]

The estimate that chance could produce a week like this every few years comes from the same kind of model, in separate runs of 200,000 simulated years. Uptime has not rated this week's failures, so the count of serious ones is this essay's judgment: three on a strict reading, five on a generous one. Pairing the strict count with Uptime's stated range, and the generous count with the higher counts implied by its recent charts, between about 15 and 55 percent of simulated years contained such a week.

The coin example assumes a fair coin and independent tosses. The birthday example uses the usual simplified assumption of 365 equally likely birthdays. The six-in-ten estimate compares an early tally, in which about half of more than 7.4 million worldwide Downdetector reports came from the United States, with roughly six million U.S. reports for the week.[14][16] The figures are rounded and use different cutoffs, so it is not an audited share.

Changing the threshold from three outages to five changes the answer greatly. These inputs do not establish how often this particular November cluster should recur. The historical totals include attacks as well as accidents.[17][18] The model illustrates chance clustering worldwide. It does not measure the probability that the particular U.S.-focused cluster was unrelated.
